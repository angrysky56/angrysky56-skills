# Claims — anchoring recommendations to checkable reality

Objective: construct claims that survive scrutiny, by anchoring them exclusively to
things a skeptic could go verify.

A defensible claim has three parts:

- **Recommendation** — the point you want accepted.
- **Grounds** — the checkable facts anchoring it.
- **Reasoning** — the bridge explaining *why* those grounds justify that point.

Most weak arguments have grounds and a recommendation with nothing between them.

## Filtering noise

Four things routinely get mistaken for evidence:

| Kind | Verdict |
|------|---------|
| **Opinion** — personal preference | Drop. Not evidence. |
| **Assertion** — claim with no backing stated | Drop. Not evidence. |
| **Anecdote** — single isolated event | Keep with warning. Cannot carry a claim alone; can illustrate one already supported. |
| **Checkable information** — data, documented observation, direct experience, attributable testimony | Keep. This is load-bearing. |

## Weighing what survives

Strength is a spectrum, not a category. Four questions, scored against *this specific
decision*:

1. **Relevant?** Does it directly touch the decision at hand — or is it merely
   about the same topic?
2. **Reliable?** Is the source trustworthy and disinterested? Who benefits if it's
   believed?
3. **Coverage?** How much of the problem does it span — the whole thing, or one
   corner generalized?
4. **Verifiable?** Can a skeptic point at it and check? If not, it's testimony about
   evidence rather than evidence.

Ten weak items do not outweigh one strong one. Volume is not weight.

## Gathering honestly

Search deliberately in both directions:

- Evidence that supports the emerging conclusion.
- **Mandatory:** evidence that would prove it wrong. Ask literally, "what would
  falsify this?" and go looking for that specifically.

This is the step that separates analysis from advocacy, and the one time pressure
quietly deletes. A conclusion that has survived a real search for disconfirmation is
far harder to knock over than one supported by ten confirmations.

## Building the bridge

The reasoning must do heavy lifting: it must answer the *strongest* objection, not the
most convenient one. Steelman the opposing case, then show why the grounds still carry
the recommendation. If you can't articulate the bridge, you have a pile of facts and a
hunch sitting adjacent to each other.

## Calibrating certainty

A wild claim with a qualifier attached is still a wild claim. Hedging doesn't buy
scope — it just makes an overreach harder to check.

Shrink the conclusion until it fits inside the evidence:

- **Scope:** "all" → "most" → "the three we measured"
- **Certainty:** "is" → "appears to" → "is consistent with"

Then stop hedging. A right-sized claim can be stated plainly, and stating it plainly
is what makes it checkable.

When the evidence doesn't reach at all, say so: "I don't know, and here's what would
settle it." That is a real answer, and often the most useful one available.

## The trigger

When asked to evaluate an argument or build a claim. Never substitute confidence for
checkable support.
