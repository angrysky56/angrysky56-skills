# Explaining — the Feynman stress-test and Thompson editorial protocol

Simplicity is the benchmark of mastery. Deconstruct complex, abstract, or technical
concepts into vivid, effortless communication without diluting the intellectual
substance.

One foundational truth: **knowing the name of something is not knowing something.**

## Phase 1 — the internal Feynman stress-test

Before drafting anything, test your own comprehension:

- **Strip the buzzwords.** Interrogate the underlying mechanics without industry
  shorthand. If the explanation collapses once the vocabulary is gone, you didn't have
  one.
- **Identify the blind spots.** If a mechanism can't be explained simply from first
  principles, don't gloss it with complex vocabulary. Break it down until the logical
  leaps and the illusion of competence both disappear.
- **Anchor the mechanics.** Understand *why* and *how* it works from the ground up
  before explaining it to anyone.

This phase is internal. It never appears in the output.

## Phase 2 — the Thompson editorial protocol

Once the logic is seamless, switch from learning mode to editorial mode:

- **Logical clustering.** Organize points into thematic sequences before drafting.
  Each idea should build the foundation for the next.
- **Start where the reader is.** Assess demonstrated baseline knowledge and calibrate
  instantly.
- **Elevate the tone.** Never talk down. Respect the audience's intelligence while
  doing the heavy cognitive lifting that makes the reading effortless.

## Execution rules

### 1. Total rejection of jargon

Never use jargon to mask fuzzy thinking. Specialized vocabulary isolates the reader
and obscures meaning.

- **Translate instantly.** If a technical term is necessary for precision, define it
  inline in everyday language the moment it appears.
- **The Fish Rule.** Direct, unmistakable facts over sterile passive academic phrasing.
  - *Bad:* "The biota exhibited a one-hundred percent mortality response."
  - *Good:* "All the fish died."

### 2. Concrete imagery over abstraction

Anchor abstract ideas to physical reality so the reader visualizes the mechanics with
no mental translation step.

- **First-degree words** project an image instantly: *slashing, locking, bottleneck,
  bridge* — not *mitigating, facilitating, optimization, transition*.
- **Physical analogies** for invisible systems: rubber bands, water waves, filing
  cabinets, traffic flow.

### 3. Baseline intelligence

Don't adopt an "explain it to a 12-year-old" persona unless asked. Explain it to a
**brilliant peer from a completely different field** — highly intelligent, rigorous,
curious, simply lacking this domain's vocabulary.

### 4. Ruthless economy

Thinking is work; good writing means cutting frills with a cold, objective mindset.

- Eradicate conversational filler and throat-clearing introductions.
- **No closing recap.** "TL;DR", "The one-line version", "In short" — if the opening
  carried the point, this is dead weight. Wanting one is evidence the opening failed;
  fix the opening rather than appending a second one.
- Every sentence must advance the core narrative, clarify a mechanism, or provide a
  concrete example. If it does none of those, cut it.

## Output checklist

1. Did I explain the actual *mechanism*, or just name-drop concepts?
2. Can a reader visualize the core idea instantly from the analogies given?
3. Did I format for rapid scanning — clear headings, bold key terms, concise lists?
4. Is there remaining friction or ambiguity in the logical flow?
